Project Schedule, Gantt Chart and Critical Path

Learn how to build a project schedule, organise activities, map dependencies, estimate durations, and explain a timeline with a Gantt chart and the Critical Path Method (CPM).

What is project scheduling?

Project scheduling is the process of defining activities, estimating how long they may take, sequencing dependencies, assigning resources where appropriate, and agreeing milestones. A project schedule turns approved scope into a time-based plan that can be monitored and updated.

How to create a project schedule

  1. Confirm the scope and deliverables. Use the approved scope statement and Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) so the schedule covers the work that must be completed.
  2. Identify activities. Break work packages into activities that can be estimated, assigned, and tracked.
  3. Sequence dependencies. Identify which activities rely on others and record the reason for each dependency.
  4. Estimate durations. Ask people familiar with the work, document assumptions, and account for uncertainty and resource availability.
  5. Add milestones. Mark important approvals, decisions, handovers, and delivery events.
  6. Review and baseline. Check logic, resource constraints, risks, and stakeholder expectations before approving the schedule baseline.
  7. Monitor and update. Record actual progress, assess impacts, and process changes through the agreed control approach.

What is a Gantt chart?

A Gantt chart is a time-based view of project activities. It commonly shows each activity as a horizontal bar across a calendar or working-time scale. It can help people see planned start and finish dates, duration, milestones, dependencies, progress, and overlapping work.

A Gantt chart is a communication and planning view; it does not automatically prove that the underlying estimates or dependencies are realistic. Keep the activity list and schedule logic accurate.

Understand activity dependencies

  • Finish-to-start (FS): the successor activity cannot start until the predecessor finishes.
  • Start-to-start (SS): the successor cannot start until the predecessor starts.
  • Finish-to-finish (FF): the successor cannot finish until the predecessor finishes.
  • Start-to-finish (SF): the successor cannot finish until the predecessor starts; this is less common.

Use dependencies that reflect real work or approval needs. Avoid adding links merely to make a schedule look detailed.

What is the Critical Path Method?

The Critical Path Method (CPM) analyses the network of activities and their durations to identify the longest-duration path through the dependency network. That path determines the shortest feasible project duration under the schedule logic and estimates used. Activities on the critical path have no total float under the relevant calculation assumptions.

A delay to a critical-path activity can delay the project finish unless the schedule is adjusted. Non-critical activities may have float, but that float can reduce as work progresses or dependencies change.

Simple project schedule example

  • Define the scope: 2 working days.
  • Gather requirements: 3 working days after scope approval.
  • Prepare the design: 4 working days after requirements.
  • Complete implementation: 5 working days after design approval.
  • Test and resolve agreed defects: 3 working days after implementation.
  • Prepare release and handover: 1 working day after testing and acceptance.

These are illustrative estimates only. A real project schedule needs a project calendar, resource availability, review times, non-working days, realistic uncertainty, and any parallel activities.

Project schedule template: useful fields

  • Activity ID and activity name
  • WBS reference and deliverable
  • Predecessor or dependency
  • Duration estimate and assumptions
  • Planned start and finish
  • Responsible owner or team
  • Milestone or approval point
  • Actual progress, forecast, and change notes

Common project scheduling mistakes

Common problems include starting with dates before scope is clear, underestimating review and approval time, ignoring resource conflicts, treating every task as independent, failing to record assumptions, and changing a baseline without impact analysis. A useful project timeline should be explainable and maintained with the people doing the work.
