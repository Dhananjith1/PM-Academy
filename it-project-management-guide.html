IT Project Management

An introductory guide to planning and coordinating technology projects, including websites, software applications, system upgrades, cloud migrations, and IT service improvements.

What is IT project management?

IT project management applies project management practices to technology-related work. An IT project manager helps clarify outcomes, coordinate people and suppliers, manage scope and dependencies, communicate decisions, monitor risks, and guide delivery through testing, release, and handover.

Technology projects often involve changing requirements, technical uncertainty, data, security, integration, and service continuity. The plan should make these concerns visible instead of treating the work as a simple list of coding tasks.

Examples of IT projects

  • Designing and launching a website or mobile application.
  • Replacing or upgrading a business information system.
  • Migrating data or workloads to a new platform.
  • Improving network, infrastructure, or IT service processes.
  • Introducing reporting, workflow automation, or a customer portal.

Typical IT project lifecycle

  1. Initiation: define the problem, intended outcomes, stakeholders, constraints, and project authority.
  2. Discovery and planning: gather requirements, assess feasibility, define scope, plan delivery, and identify risks and dependencies.
  3. Design: agree user experience, solution design, interfaces, data needs, and acceptance criteria with relevant specialists.
  4. Build or configure: coordinate development, configuration, data preparation, supplier work, and incremental reviews.
  5. Testing and acceptance: plan quality checks, integration testing, user acceptance, defect handling, and approval.
  6. Release and transition: coordinate deployment readiness, communications, training, support arrangements, and rollback planning where appropriate.
  7. Closure: confirm acceptance, transfer ownership, close actions, document lessons, and review outcomes.

Project management and the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

The Software Development Life Cycle describes activities involved in creating, changing, testing, releasing, and maintaining software. Project management coordinates the overall work, people, constraints, decisions, risks, and outcomes. The two are connected but not identical: a project may use an SDLC approach while still needing its own governance, budget, stakeholder, schedule, and change controls.

Important IT project management areas

  • Requirements and scope: record user needs, acceptance criteria, exclusions, and how changes will be reviewed.
  • Backlog and prioritisation: organise work items, clarify value, dependencies, and readiness.
  • Technical dependencies: track integrations, environments, access, data migration, suppliers, and architecture decisions.
  • Security and privacy: involve the appropriate specialists early and follow organisational policies and applicable requirements.
  • Quality and testing: plan test ownership, environments, defect triage, and acceptance.
  • Release and operations: confirm service ownership, support, monitoring, documentation, and handover.

Agile, predictive, and hybrid IT delivery

Agile delivery can help teams learn through increments and frequent feedback when requirements evolve. Predictive planning may be appropriate when scope, approvals, or dependencies need more upfront coordination. Hybrid delivery combines approaches. Choose based on uncertainty, risk, integration, regulation, and stakeholder needs—not because a method is fashionable.

IT project manager roles and responsibilities

Responsibilities vary by organisation, but commonly include maintaining the project plan, coordinating contributors, facilitating updates and decisions, tracking schedule and budget, maintaining risk and issue records, escalating blockers, controlling changes, and supporting testing, release, and handover. Technical decisions should be made with the responsible technical specialists.

Useful IT project management documents

Common artefacts include a project charter, requirements log, WBS, delivery roadmap, sprint or work board, RAID log, decision log, test and acceptance plan, release checklist, communication plan, and handover record. Use only the documents that genuinely help the team coordinate and control the work.
